title: How to Make a Video Look Hand-Drawn
desc: The four effects that separate hand-made animation from digital video, and the order to apply them in so the result looks drawn rather than filtered.
answer: To make a video look hand-drawn, add line boil so the edges are redrawn each frame, hold each drawing for two frames instead of one, split the color channels slightly, and lay a fine grain over the whole thing.
date: 2026-08-16
---
Most attempts to make footage look hand-made reach for a texture overlay and stop there. A paper texture on top of mathematically perfect motion still reads as digital, because the giveaway was never the surface. It was the stability.

Four things carry the impression, roughly in order of how much they matter.

## 1. The lines have to move

This is the one that does most of the work. In hand-drawn animation every frame is a fresh drawing, so every outline lands a little off from where it was. Adding that wander back is called line boil, and without it the other three effects are decoration on a corpse.

## 2. The frame rate has to feel drawn

Hand-drawn animation is usually shot on twos: one drawing held for two frames, twelve drawings a second instead of twenty-four. It is a budget decision that became a style, and audiences read it as animation.

Holding the boil for two frames reproduces the rhythm without touching the actual frame rate of the clip. Hold for one and it buzzes. Hold for two and it breathes.

## 3. The color should be slightly out of register

Printed comics and film prints never had their color layers land perfectly on top of each other. Pulling the red and blue channels a pixel or two apart imitates that misregistration.

Keep it small. Two or three pixels reads as a physical process. Ten pixels reads as a 3D movie without the glasses.

## 4. Grain last, and less than you think

Grain sells the idea that light passed through something physical. It belongs on top of everything else, because in the real process it was the last thing that happened.

The failure mode is volume. Grain heavy enough to notice on a still frame is far too heavy in motion, where it crawls and pulls attention off the drawing.

## The order matters

Apply these in the wrong order and the seams show.

| Order | Step | Why here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Line boil | It has to happen to the artwork, not to a grainy copy of it |
| 2 | Frame hold | Part of the boil, not a separate pass |
| 3 | Channel split | Imitates a printing step, which came after the drawing |
| 4 | Grain | Imitates film, which came after the printing |

A channel split applied before the boil gets displaced along with the edges, which smears the fringing into the wobble and looks like a compression artifact rather than a print.

## What not to add

Skip the paper texture unless the piece genuinely lives on paper. It is the most reached-for and least convincing move, because real animation cels were photographed, not printed onto stock. A vignette has the same problem: it says lens, and lenses are what you are trying to get away from.

Boiler does all four of these in one pass, in this order, and keeps the original audio.
